How the NAMM Foundation's 2021 Music Education Report Changed What We Ask of Schools

Laura Kim reflects on the NAMM Foundation's landmark 2021 music education report and what it meant for teachers, students, and the future of music access.

What the NAMM Foundation Actually Said in January 2021

The Believe in Music event was virtual by necessity, but it served as a platform for some serious data. The NAMM Foundation, working alongside SupportMusic.com, had been tracking school music program cuts throughout 2020. Their findings, presented during the event, showed that music programs were among the first to be reduced or suspended when budgets tightened under pandemic strain. This wasn't surprising to anyone in the field, but having it documented and publicized at scale was different from the anecdotal picture most of us were living inside.

According to the NAMM Foundation, music instruction has documented links to improved reading skills, math performance, and student engagement, and these benefits are disproportionately significant for students from low-income households. Separately, a widely cited 2019 study from the University of British Columbia, referenced in coverage by Education Week, found that students who participated in school music programs showed measurably stronger academic outcomes compared to peers without music access, with effects compounding over multiple years of participation. The 2021 NAMM presentation used this body of research to argue that cutting music wasn't a neutral budget decision. It was a choice with academic consequences.

The event also spotlighted a policy angle that I think got underreported. Advocates used the Believe in Music platform to push for music education's inclusion in federal pandemic relief funding, specifically the Elementary and Secondary School Emergency Relief (ESSER) funds made available through the CARES Act and its successors. That was a genuinely strategic move, and some districts did eventually use ESSER money to restore or protect their music programs.

Why Music Teachers Were Already at a Breaking Point Before This

Here's something I want you to understand if you didn't live it from inside a music classroom: the pandemic didn't create the vulnerability of school music programs. It exposed it. In my 20-plus years of teaching, I'd watched music, arts, and physical education get treated as the soft perimeter of a school's curriculum, the first things trimmed when money gets tight and the last things restored when it loosens. The 2021 NAMM report essentially said the same thing in more diplomatic language.

Remote learning was a particular cruelty for instrumental instruction. A student can read a text or watch a math video asynchronously. But playing a wind instrument over a video call produces latency that makes ensemble playing impossible, and many families didn't have a piano or keyboard at home. I had students in early 2021 who were sight-reading on a printed keyboard diagram taped to a table. It worked, sort of, in the same way that shadow boxing works if you're trying to learn how to punch. It keeps the muscle memory moving. But it's not the same thing.

If you're curious about how foundational sight-reading skills fit into a student's early musical development, especially under constrained conditions, the approach I describe in The 15-Minute Daily Routine That Actually Builds Sight-Reading Fluency is worth reading alongside this context. Short, consistent, structured practice became a lifeline for a lot of students in 2021 who had nothing else.

The Reaction From Educators, and Where It Got Complicated

Okay, I'm going to be honest with you for a second, and this is the part where I'm less sure of myself than the rest of this piece. The NAMM Foundation is, at its core, an industry organization. It represents instrument manufacturers, retailers, and the broader music products industry. Their advocacy for music education is genuine, I believe that, but it's also not fully separable from the commercial interest in people buying instruments. My friend Rachel, who teaches strings at a public middle school in Chicago, put it to me this way on a phone call around March 2021: "I'm glad they're saying the right things, but I wonder who they're really talking to." She wasn't being cynical exactly, just... realistic. And I think she had a point. The educators who most needed support in January 2021 weren't necessarily the ones tuning in to a virtual trade event. For more on this, see How the 2022 NAMM Music Education Report Changed What We Thought We Knew About Student Access.

That said, the reaction within the music education community was largely positive. Organizations like NAfME (National Association for Music Education) amplified the messaging. State music education associations shared the data in their advocacy materials. And some of the coverage reached school board members and district administrators who genuinely hadn't seen the research laid out so plainly before.

The misconception I'd push back on, though, is the idea that data alone changes institutional behavior. Districts don't restore music programs because a report says they should. They do it when parents organize, when teachers persist, and when funding becomes available. The NAMM report gave advocates a better argument. It didn't give them a faster school board.

What This Moment Means for How We Teach Sight-Reading and Musical Literacy

There's a specific lesson buried in the 2021 moment that I think music educators should hold onto. When programs are at risk and resources are thin, the skills that survive are the ones that transfer. A student who can read music independently, who can look at a page and hear something in their head before their fingers move, carries that skill with them even without a teacher in the room. That's not an argument against ensemble programs or in-person instruction. It's an argument for building musical literacy as a foundation, not a luxury.

According to the National Endowment for the Arts, students with access to music education in low-income schools show stronger reading comprehension and attendance rates than peers without that access. That kind of evidence, paired with the NAMM Foundation's 2021 advocacy, made a stronger case than either could alone. But the practical upshot for teachers is this: build transferable skills early and deliberately. Sight-reading is one of them. So is rhythmic independence, which tends to be neglected even when pitch gets appropriate attention. If you want to understand why that gap exists, Why Rhythm Is Harder to Fix Than Pitch in Sight-Reading gets into the mechanics of that problem clearly.

For students who did stick with lessons through 2021, remotely or otherwise, the ones who made the most progress were those who'd been taught to work independently with notated music. I saw this pattern consistently across my own students. The kids who could sit with a piece of sheet music and make sense of it on their own had something to do in the gaps between lessons. The ones who relied entirely on demonstration and imitation were lost. That observation shaped how I think about early music education in a permanent way. And if you're teaching piano specifically, the approach outlined in How to Sight-Read Chord Progressions at the Piano: A Conservatory Method That Actually Works is the kind of skill-building that pays off exactly in moments like 2021.

If you're a music teacher, a parent advocating for your child's school program, or an administrator making budget decisions right now: print out the NAMM Foundation's 2021 findings, bring them to your next school board meeting, and pair them with local data on your district's program cuts. The research exists. The case is already made. Your job is to put it in front of the people who need to hear it, and to keep building musical literacy skills in your students that no budget cut can take away from them once they're learned.
