**Title:** Is your sight-reading practice teaching you to stop every time you miss a note?

Something we keep seeing after years of teaching drums, piano and violin: most students who say they practice sight-reading are really practicing stopping.

The pattern goes like this. They pick a piece that is a bit too hard. They hit a wrong note, stop, fix it, and start the bar again. Do that fifty times a week and you build a reflex where a mistake means you stop. Then in a rehearsal or audition the reflex fires and the whole read falls apart.

What has worked better for our students:

- Read material easy enough to get through without stopping. If you stop more than once or twice a page, it is too hard.
- Keep the pulse no matter what. If you miss a note, let it go and land on the next beat.
- Never read the same page twice in one session. The second pass is memory, not reading.

I once recorded myself reading a stack of Bach chorales and the stops came way more often than I expected. Hearing the playback was humbling.

Do you deliberately train through mistakes, or do you fix things as you go?

## LINKEDIN
**Hook:** Most musicians who practice sight-reading are actually practicing how to stop.

Every time a player hits a wrong note, stops and fixes it, they strengthen one link: a mistake, then a stop. Months of that and the reflex shows up exactly where it costs the most, in rehearsals, auditions and gigs.

Our faculty teaches drums, piano and violin, and the fix is the same on all three. Choose material easy enough to play straight through. Keep the pulse moving when a note goes wrong. Never read the same page twice in one session, because the second pass is memory, not reading.

It feels like lowering the bar. It actually trains the one skill that matters on a real music stand: recovering and carrying on.

The same idea works outside music. When you practice a skill with constant restarts, you get good at restarting, not at the skill.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why am I not getting better at sight-reading even though I practice it every day?

Daily practice that doesn't improve your sight-reading usually means you are practicing a different skill than you think. For most players, that skill is stopping.

Here is the usual pattern. You open a piece a little above your level, play until the first wrong note, stop, fix it and restart the phrase. That feels responsible. But your brain learns from what you repeat, and you are repeating a loop where a mistake means you stop and correct. After a few months the reflex is automatic. Then at a rehearsal or audition, where stopping isn't allowed, it fires anyway and the read falls apart.

We teach drums, piano and violin, and three changes make the biggest difference on all of them:

1. Read easier music than you think you need. If you can't get through a page with at most one or two hesitations, it is too hard for sight-reading practice. Keep that piece for repertoire work.

2. Keep the pulse no matter what. Set a slow tempo, and if you miss a note, drop it and land on the next beat. A wrong note in time beats a right note late. This trains your eyes to stay ahead of your hands.

3. Never read the same page twice in one session. By the second pass you are partly playing from memory, and that is no longer sight-reading. Volume of new material is what builds the skill.

It also helps to record yourself. Most players stop far more often than they realise, and the playback makes the habit obvious. We wrote up the full method, including how one faculty member caught this in their own Bach chorale reading, here: https://siavashsed.github.io/sightreadingacademy/sight-reading-without-training-the-stop-reflex/

Give it three or four weeks of easy, unbroken, always-new reading. Most students notice the difference in their first ensemble rehearsal.
