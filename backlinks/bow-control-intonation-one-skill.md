## LINKEDIN
**Hook:** Your tuner says you're in tune. Your bow arm may be quietly saying otherwise.

Last March, one of our students drilled a D major scale for forty minutes with a clip-on tuner. Every note read green at a soft dynamic. The moment she dug in at the frog, the pitch drifted sharp.

Her fingers hadn't moved. The bow had changed the note.

Most string students practice intonation and bow control as separate problems. That split is where a lot of practice time gets wasted: you fix the left hand, then the right arm undoes it as soon as the dynamic changes.

The fix isn't more repetition. It's practicing the same passage at several dynamics with consistent bow speed and contact point, and training your ear to tell which hand caused the error.

It's a good reminder for any skill: when a problem keeps coming back, check whether you're solving the right one.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** Why does my violin go out of tune when I play louder, even though my finger placement is correct?

This is one of the most common frustrations we see in students, and the cause is usually not your left hand.

When you play louder, you almost always add bow weight, slow the bow down, or move the contact point closer to the bridge or deeper into the frog. Each of those changes how the string vibrates. Pressing harder stretches the string slightly and raises the pitch, so a note that was dead centre on the tuner at a soft dynamic can read sharp at a loud one. Your finger hasn't moved, but the sounding pitch has.

Here's how to separate the two problems:

1. Play a slow scale with a tuner at a soft dynamic and note where each pitch sits.
2. Repeat it at medium and loud dynamics without changing finger placement. If the tuner readings shift, the bow is involved.
3. Keep bow speed and contact point steady and build volume mostly through speed rather than pressing down. Many students over-press without realising it.
4. Pay special attention near the frog, where the bow is naturally heavier and it's easiest to dig in.
5. Practice open strings at different dynamics first. With no finger involved, any pitch change is clearly coming from the bow.

One of our students spent forty minutes on a D major scale with every note reading green at a soft dynamic, then went sharp the moment she dug in near the frog. Once she treated bow control and intonation as one skill rather than two, her tuning became far more stable across dynamics.
