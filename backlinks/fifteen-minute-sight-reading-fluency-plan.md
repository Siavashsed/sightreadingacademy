**Title:** Does stopping to fix wrong notes actually make your sight reading worse?

I spent three weeks in autumn 2022 working through a Hal Leonard Grade 3 book on an upright Yamaha, with a kitchen timer set for fifteen minutes. Every time I hit a wrong note I stopped, went back and fixed it. My reading got worse, not better. It only started improving when I made one rule: never stop, never go back, even if the passage falls apart.

The reason, as I understand it, is that sight reading is a skill of keeping time and looking ahead, not a skill of accuracy. Stopping trains you to read one bar at a time and to treat mistakes as emergencies. Playing through trains your eyes to stay a beat or two ahead of your hands, which is the thing fluent readers actually do.

The second thing that surprised me was the schedule. Fifteen minutes every day did more for me than one long session on Sunday, because the skill is more about pattern recognition than muscle, and it fades quickly between sessions.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How long should I practice sight reading each day to actually get better?

For most students, fifteen minutes a day is enough, and it will do more for you than a single hour on the weekend. The reason is that sight reading is mostly pattern recognition: your eyes learn to group notes into shapes, intervals and rhythms, and that kind of learning fades quickly if you leave long gaps between sessions. Short, daily contact keeps the patterns fresh.

But the minutes matter less than how you spend them. The most important rule is this: never stop to fix a wrong note. When you stop and go back, you train yourself to read one bar at a time and to treat every error as an emergency. In a real performance or ensemble, nobody waits for you. Playing through mistakes trains the skill you actually need, which is keeping steady time while your eyes stay a beat or two ahead of your hands.

A simple daily routine looks like this:

1. Choose music clearly easier than what you normally play, ideally one or two grades below.
2. Take ten seconds to check the key signature, time signature and any obvious rhythm traps.
3. Set a slow, steady tempo and keep it, no matter what happens.
4. Play the piece once through without stopping. Miss notes, keep going.
5. Move on to a new piece the next day rather than polishing the same one.

Variety matters, because repeating one piece trains memory, not reading. Fresh material every day is what builds fluency.
