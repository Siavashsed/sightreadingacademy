**Title:** Most missed shifts in a cold read aren't a technique problem. They're late decisions.

Something I've noticed after years of sitting in reading sessions and teaching students to read: most of the missed shifts I hear aren't a technique problem. They're a timing-of-the-decision problem. The player sees the high note, panics, and shifts on the beat where the note lands, usually mid-slur.

What's helped me and my students the most:

1. Pick the shift point at least a bar early. Your eyes should already be past it.
2. Shift on an open string, a rest, or a repeated note whenever the part gives you one. The open string buys you a free half second.
3. Default to third position for passages that hover around the A and E strings. A lot of orchestral and chamber writing just lives there, so you shift less often in the first place.

I still blow this myself. Last spring I missed the same climb into fifth three times in a row on a Dvořák part I was reading cold, because I kept deciding too late.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** How do I handle position shifts when sight-reading on violin?

The short answer: decide your shifts early and put them where the music gives you free time. Most shifts that go wrong in a cold read are not a technique failure. The hand knows how to get to fifth position. The decision to go there just arrived on the same beat as the note.

Here is what we teach our students at Sight Reading Academy.

1. Read ahead, then commit. Your eyes should be at least a bar past your bow. When you spot a passage that climbs, pick the exact note where you will shift before you reach it, and do not revise that choice mid-phrase.

2. Shift on free time. An open string, a rest or a repeated note lets the hand move while something else is sounding or nothing needs to sound. Shifting in the middle of a slurred stepwise run is where most cracks and wrong notes come from.

3. Make third position your home base. A lot of orchestral, chamber and etude writing sits in a range that third position covers without moving. If you start a passage there instead of in first, you often remove two or three shifts entirely.

4. Practice the decision, not just the motion. Take unfamiliar parts, give yourself ten seconds to scan, pencil in your shift points, then play once without stopping. Afterwards, check which shifts failed and whether the choice or the execution was the cause. Usually it is the choice.

Get the timing of the decision right and the technique usually takes care of itself.
