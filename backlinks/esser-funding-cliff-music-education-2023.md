## LINKEDIN
**Hook:** One-time federal money paid for recurring costs in thousands of school music rooms.

Between 2021 and 2024, the American Rescue Plan sent $122 billion in ESSER funds to schools, and every dollar had to be committed by September 30, 2024. Many music programs used their share for instruments, staff and software they could not have afforded otherwise.

The catch is in how the money worked. Relief money is one-time. Software licenses, instrument repairs and salaries are not. When the funding ends, each of those lines either moves into the general budget or quietly disappears.

We are working musicians who teach. The most useful thing a music educator or arts administrator can do is make a list of every item in the program that relief dollars paid for, note when each one renews, and bring that list to budget meetings before cuts are proposed, not after.

Programs that see the cliff early have options. Programs that find out in June usually do not.

## QUORA
**Answer this question:** What happens to school music programs now that ESSER funding has ended?

We teach drums, piano and violin at Sight Reading Academy, and a lot of our students come through public school music programs, so we follow this question closely.

Short version: anything a music program paid for with ESSER money has to find a new funding source or it goes away. ESSER was federal pandemic relief. The largest round, from the American Rescue Plan, was $122 billion, and districts had to commit those funds by September 30, 2024. It was always one-time money.

Music programs used it in a few common ways:

- Instruments: loaner instruments, replacements for worn out school instruments, sometimes whole new sections.
- Staff: extra teachers or aides to reduce class sizes or restart programs that had paused.
- Software: notation, practice and assessment tools licensed per student, per year.

Instruments last the longest of the three, but they still need repairs, strings, drumheads and reeds. Software and staff cost money every year by definition. When the relief money ends, those costs either move into the district's general budget or get cut, and music is often near the front of the line when budgets tighten.

If you are a parent or teacher, here are the practical steps:

1. Ask the director or principal which program items were ESSER funded.
2. Find out what has already moved into the regular budget and what has not.
3. Go to the budget hearings before decisions are final, not after.
4. Look to booster clubs, local arts foundations and state arts grants to cover specific items.

We wrote a longer piece on how this plays out in school music rooms, including what to look for in a district budget: https://siavashsed.github.io/sightreadingacademy/esser-funding-cliff-music-education-2023/

The programs that survive funding cliffs are usually the ones where someone noticed early and spoke up.
