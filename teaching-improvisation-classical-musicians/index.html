Classical Musicians Can Improvise. They Just Don't Know It Yet.

Professor Laura Kim on why classical musicians freeze when asked to improvise, and the practical steps that actually break through that wall.

Photo: cottonbro studio
By Laura Kim · July 01, 2026 · Music Education · 5 min read

Most classical musicians can sight-read a Brahms intermezzo at tempo, transpose in their heads, and hear a wrong note from across a rehearsal room. Ask them to play four bars of anything they made up themselves and they go completely still. I've watched this happen dozens of times, but the moment that stays with me was a junior piano student at a summer intensive in 2017. She was technically one of the strongest students in the room, probably top three. I asked her to improvise a short ending to a phrase I played. She sat at the Steinway D for almost forty-five seconds in total silence, then quietly said, "I don't know how." She did know how. She just didn't believe she was allowed.

That belief, that improvisation is a different skill entirely, something jazz musicians are born with and classical players are permanently excluded from, is almost entirely a product of how we train people. It's a pedagogical artifact, not a musical reality. And the research backs this up: a 2013 study published in Psychology of Music found that classical musicians show significantly higher performance anxiety around open-ended creative tasks than their peers in other genres, largely because their training rarely includes unscripted performance. The anxiety isn't evidence of inability. It's evidence of a gap in their education. Our job is to close it.

Why Classical Training Accidentally Teaches Fear of the Wrong Note

Classical pedagogy is, in many ways, a long lesson in precision. Every marking on the page matters. Fingerings are corrected, articulations refined, dynamic shadings adjusted until they match the score, the editor's intentions, the teacher's interpretation. That kind of attention to detail produces extraordinary musicians. It also produces people who have been trained, for years, to see any deviation from the written text as an error.

Improvisation requires the opposite reflex. There are no wrong notes in the same way, because there's no text to be wrong about. But if you've spent a decade having your deviations corrected, your instinct when you sit down to create freely is to freeze. The brain keeps scanning for the right answer. And there isn't one. That scan never resolves, so nothing comes out.

In my work with undergraduate piano students at the conservatory level, I've seen this pattern so consistently that I'd almost call it universal. The students who struggle most with improvisation are often the ones with the most technically advanced reading skills. The notation has become their security blanket, and we have to gently pull it away. Teaching theory through improvisation rather than workbooks is one of the fastest ways to start doing that, because it reframes theory as a tool for creation rather than a rule system for judgment.

Starting Smaller Than You Think You Need To

The biggest mistake I made early in my teaching was starting improvisational exercises at the phrase level. Eight bars, a clear beginning and end, some kind of shape. It seemed modest to me. For students who had never improvised, it was terrifying. Eight bars is an eternity when you have no map.

So I scaled down. Way down. The first exercise I now use is what I call a "one-decision" prompt. Play any single note. Then play a second note that feels like it belongs next to it. Stop. That's it. No rhythm requirement, no key, no duration. Just two notes. It sounds almost insultingly simple, and the first time I tried it in a group lesson, one of my students actually laughed. But here's the thing... every single person in that room played their two notes. Nobody froze. And for several of them, that was the first time they'd ever made a purely spontaneous musical decision at an instrument.

From there, you build incrementally. Two notes become a three-note fragment. The fragment gets a rhythm. The rhythm gets a shape. By the time students are playing eight-bar phrases, they've accumulated dozens of small successful decisions, and the fear has somewhere real to land. The National Association for Music Education has emphasized since their 2014 standards revision that improvisation should appear across all grade levels and instruments, precisely because starting late makes the barrier almost insurmountable.

The Pentatonic Trick, the Drone, and Why Constraints Are Your Best Friend

Constraints feel counterintuitive when you're trying to encourage freedom. But total freedom is paralyzing. Giving a student a pentatonic scale and a drone in the bass is not limiting their creativity. It's giving them a safe floor to stand on while they build the muscle of spontaneous decision-making.

The pentatonic scale works so well for early improvisation because it has no half-steps. Every note sounds relatively consonant against a tonic drone, which means students get immediate positive sensory feedback from their choices. They play something. It sounds okay. They play something else. It also sounds okay. That feedback loop, rather than the loop of "was that right or wrong," is what starts to break down the freeze response.

I'm not 100% sure this works for everyone, but... okay, a slightly embarrassing confession. My friend and fellow faculty member David tried the drone exercise with his adult cello students and told me it "felt too much like meditation class" and several of them just sat there looking serene and not playing anything. So maybe the drone is a piano-specific thing, or maybe David needs to tell people more firmly to actually play something. I genuinely don't know. We argued about it over bad coffee in the faculty lounge for longer than either of us should admit. The point is, the constraint principle holds even if the specific tool needs adjusting for your instrument or your students.

Other useful constraints: limit students to only the black keys for two minutes. Give them a rhythm pattern in one hand and ask them to invent a melody in the other. Ask them to play only three different pitches but make it sound like a whole piece. Each constraint removes one dimension of decision-making and lets the student concentrate on another.

Making Improvisation a Regular Part of Lessons, Not a Special Event

The students who make the fastest progress at improvisation are the ones whose teachers treat it as unremarkable. When improvisation appears in every lesson, even for five minutes, even as a warm-up, it stops being a high-stakes audition for creative ability. It becomes just another thing we do at the piano.

In practice, I spend roughly the last ten minutes of each lesson on some form of free play. Sometimes I give a prompt, "use only minor seconds and see what happens." Sometimes I ask the student to improvise a short introduction to the piece we've been working on. Sometimes I just say, "play something you didn't plan." The content matters less than the consistency. Research by Ericsson, Krampe, and Tesch-Romer (1993) in Psychological Review established that deliberate, repeated practice of a specific skill is what drives competence, and improvisation is a skill like any other. It responds to training.

What I'd push back on is the idea that classical musicians need to "become" improvisers in some identity-level way before they can actually do it. That framing is too heavy. You don't need a new identity. You need practice and permission. The permission is yours to give them, and you should give it early, often, and without making a big deal of it.

  • Five-minute rule: Reserve the last five to ten minutes of every lesson for unscripted play, no grading, no correction during the playing itself.
  • Record it: Have students record their improvisations on their phone. Listening back builds self-awareness without the pressure of performing.
  • Separate the making from the judging: Don't give feedback during the improvisation itself. Let it finish first. Then ask the student what they noticed.

Pick one student this week, ideally one who freezes the hardest, and try the two-note exercise. Don't frame it as improvisation. Just say, "play any note, then play one that feels right next to it." See what happens. The barrier usually isn't as solid as it looks from the outside, and one small success tends to open a crack that you can work with for months.

Frequently asked questions

Can classical musicians actually learn to improvise as adults?

Yes, and the research on deliberate practice supports this clearly. The barrier for adult classical musicians is primarily psychological and pedagogical, not neurological. Consistent, low-pressure practice of small improvisational decisions builds the skill over time, most students show noticeable progress within eight to twelve weeks of regular work.

What is the best scale to start improvisation for a beginner classical pianist?

The pentatonic scale is the most forgiving starting point because every note sounds consonant against a simple tonic drone. There are no half-steps, so accidental clashes are minimized and the student gets positive feedback from their choices immediately. The black keys on a piano already form a pentatonic scale, so you can tell a student to use only those and they're ready to go in under a minute.

How do I introduce improvisation to a student who is resistant or anxious about it?

Start with constraints so small they barely register as improvisation. Ask for one note, then a second note that feels right next to it. Don't use the word 'improvise' if it's carrying too much weight. Frame it as 'free play' or 'an experiment.' Remove evaluation during the playing itself, let it finish before you respond, and keep the initial prompts open-ended enough that there's genuinely no wrong answer.
